MUR M26

Firmware and telemetry for an electric Formula SAE race car

Every year, Melbourne University Racing designs and builds a new car to compete at Formula SAE Australasia. For the M26, I led a team of 7 to develop the car's software from the ground up, including the firmware on the car and a telemetry service to send its data back to the pits.

01 · The brief

The M26 is an electric race car made up of several controllers that communicate with each other over CAN. Our team is responsible for the firmware running on these embedded nodes, the CAN communication between them, and a wireless telemetry link so the team can see live data from the car.

All of this was built from scratch with a hard deadline, as the car competes at Formula SAE-A in Tailem Bend, South Australia in December 2026.

02 · My role

As team lead, I split my time between engineering work and managing the project to keep it on schedule.

  • ECU logic. I was responsible for the ECU logic, written in C on Zephyr RTOS.

  • Integration. I handled the final integration of all components into a single monorepo, so the software for the whole car builds together.

  • Project management. I managed the project across the team, working with and around the other sub-teams to meet tight deadlines, since a lot of our work depends on their hardware.

03 · Architecture

The car's controllers communicate over CAN, with the ECU firmware coordinating them. A telemetry service then sends data over a wireless link to a ground station in the pits.

04 · Challenges

First time on embedded

This was my first time working with embedded systems. Before the M26 I had only written software for regular computers, so working with an RTOS, real-time constraints and hardware that wasn't always ready yet took some getting used to.

How did you get up to speed? A specific bug you tracked down on the real hardware would fit well here.

Integrating with the rest of the car

Our software depends on hardware from almost every other sub-team, so it's often one of the last parts of the car to come together. To stay on schedule, we had to work with and around their timelines instead of waiting for them.

What did the team do to work around this? e.g. mocked CAN traffic, bench testing, or agreeing on interfaces early.

Getting hands-on

The role also took me out of my comfort zone. During manufacturing I learnt to solder, machine parts and work safely around high voltage systems, which gave me a much better understanding of the hardware our code runs on.
