MUR M26

Firmware and telemetry for an electric Formula SAE race car

Melbourne University Racing designs and builds a new car each year to compete at Formula SAE Australasia. For the M26, I led the team writing the car's software from the ground up: the firmware on the car itself and the telemetry service that streams its data back to the pits.

01 · The brief

An electric race car is a set of separate controllers that all have to agree, in real time, about what the car is doing. The software team owns the code that makes that happen. That covers firmware running on the car's embedded nodes, the CAN bus communication tying them together, and a wireless telemetry link so the team can see live data from the car.

The M26's software was a ground-up build, with a hard deadline: the car competes at Formula SAE-A in Tailem Bend, South Australia, in December 2026.

02 · My role

I led a team of seven, and split my time between engineering and making sure the work landed on schedule.

  • ECU logic. I was responsible for the ECU logic, written in C on Zephyr RTOS.

  • Integration. I owned the final integration of every component into a single monorepo, so the whole car's software builds and ships together.

  • Project management. I ran the team-wide plan, working with and around the other sub-teams (whose hardware our code depends on) to hit tight deadlines.

03 · Architecture

At a high level, the car's controllers talk over CAN. The ECU firmware coordinates them, and a telemetry service forwards data over a wireless link to a ground station in the pits.

04 · Challenges

First time on embedded

Before the M26 I'd only written software for computers with an OS and plenty of memory. On the car, it's an RTOS, a real-time deadline, hardware that may not exist yet, and bugs that look like electrical faults (and vice versa).

How did you get up to speed? A specific bug you chased down on real hardware is a great story here.

Integrating with the rest of the car

Software is the last thing to come together on a race car, because it depends on every other sub-team's hardware being ready. Keeping the schedule meant working with and around their timelines rather than waiting on them.

What did you do concretely? E.g. mocked CAN traffic, bench rigs, or an interface spec agreed up front.

Getting hands-on

Leading software on a race team also meant leaving the keyboard. During manufacturing I learned to solder, machine parts and work under high-voltage safety procedures, which made me a much better judge of where a problem actually lives.
